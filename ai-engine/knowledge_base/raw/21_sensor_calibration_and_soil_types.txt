Soil Moisture Sensor Calibration and Soil Types

Moisture percentages from a sensor are not automatically comparable across sensors, soils or installations. Do not treat one percentage as a universal rule.

What the sensor percentage means: in this system the sensor value is converted from the raw analog (ADC) reading into a percentage. Unless it has been calibrated against measured soil samples, that percentage is a device-specific relative scale (dry end to wet end of that sensor), not true volumetric water content. Low-cost capacitive sensors commonly give a raw value that falls as moisture rises, and the usable range depends on the sensor model, supply voltage and ADC resolution (for example 0 to 1023 or 0 to 4095). Check the datasheet of your sensor for its measurement range.

How to calibrate: record the raw reading with the sensor dry (in air or oven-dry soil) and again in saturated soil or water, then map those two values to 0 and 100 percent. For better accuracy compare readings with soil samples from the same field, because each soil type gives a slightly different curve. Recalibrate if the sensor is replaced, moved, or the soil changes.

Soil type and thresholds (approximate volumetric values, FAO-56 Table 19): sand holds field capacity at about 7 to 17 percent and wilting point at about 2 to 7 percent. Loam holds about 20 to 30 percent at field capacity and 7 to 17 percent at wilting point. Clay holds about 32 to 40 percent at field capacity and 20 to 24 percent at wilting point. Black cotton soils (vertisols) are clay-rich, hold large amounts of water, crack when dry and drain slowly when wet.

What this means for the advisory thresholds: the default thresholds of this system (critical below 15 percent, dry below 25 percent, wet above 35 percent) suit a medium loam-like soil. In sandy soil, a reading near 25 percent may already mean very wet soil. In clay or black soil, a reading near 25 percent may be close to the stress zone because plants cannot use water held tightly by clay. Always tell the farmer that thresholds depend on soil type, and ask for the soil type if it matters to the answer.

When the sensor conflicts with the field: first check the sensor depth (it should sit in the root zone, about 10 to 30 cm) and that it has firm soil contact with no air gap. Then do a hand check: dig 15 to 20 cm and squeeze a handful of soil; it should hold together without dripping. Look at the crop: wilting in the afternoon that recovers by evening is early stress. Compare with a second reading hours later or at another spot, and check wiring, power and corrosion. If the checked field condition and the sensor disagree, trust the verified field observation, recalibrate the sensor, and treat the advisory as guidance only.
